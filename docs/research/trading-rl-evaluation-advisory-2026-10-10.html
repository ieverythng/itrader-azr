1 · Why the R4-v1 vs R0 gap is not a promotion (Point 1)

User framing: "We have not done heavy proper training for a while — we have been correcting reporting, PBT, setups, and running short ablations to stress-test the system. Only recently did we start pushing toward longer sets + the now-official R4-v1 to reduce the POMDP and push closer to MDP. Hence, is it worth pushing R0 further with a larger sample size, validating with the much lower bp cost, and continuing from there?"

Answer: yes — and that is the correct scientific move, before promoting R4-v1.

  1. The 4.4 bp gap is dominated by cost noise, not signal. At evidence_cost_bps=1.0, a 60–200 bar episode already pays 0.06–0.20% in cost if the model changes position even a handful of times. A 4.4 bp improvement is inside that cost budget. Until we show the architecture survives at lower cost and generalizes across seeds, the 4.4 bp is a curiosity, not an edge.
  2. We are currently under-trained relative to the question we are asking. The current loadout is iters=40, train_batch_size=4800 → ≈192,000 sampled environment steps. That is a smoke / attribution budget, not a training budget. For a directional Discrete(3) policy on a 1-minute bar dataset with ~5.5M RTH rows, the honest training budget is 10–100× that — on the order of 1.9M–19M+ steps, run on the frozen 70/15/15 split, multi-seed, and compared head-to-head with R0.
  3. "Push R0 further, then R4-v1" is the right order, not the reverse. The masterplan already says: keep R0 as the immutable parity control and run R1 beside a matched Ray-2.58 R0 control. The same principle applies to R4-v1: we should establish the R0 ceiling under the larger training budget and the lower-cost regime first, so that when R4-v1 is tested the comparison is not confounded by "R4-v1 got more training" or "R4-v1 was tested at a different cost."
  4. The lower-bp cost validation is the key gate. If R0 (and R4-v1) both become substantially more profitable at 0.1 bp / 0.01 bp than at 1 bp, we have learned something important: the model was trading at all and the 1 bp cost was masking the signal. If R0 is still flat or negative at 0.01 bp, the architecture is the bottleneck, not the cost — and we should keep iterating on the solver (R1/R2c) rather than the cost knob.

2 · PPO training cadence — per-episode or per-step? (Point 3)

User question: "Is PPO done per episode? Of course there are plenty of SGD steps in between, but that 1 bp cost was arbitrary though tested continually. Would 0.1 bp or even 0.01 bp really constrain the model enough? … I want to ensure we are punishing overtrading properly!"

What the code actually does (grounding):

  • itrader_env.py — reward is computed per step, not per episode. The net_return objective accumulates per-step mark-to-market PnL minus configured costs.
  • PPO's GAE (generalized advantage estimation) is computed over the episode using gamma∈{0.98,0.99,0.995}. So the credit assignment is per-episode, but the reward is per-step.
  • The 1 bp cost is charged per round-trip (each position change), not per bar. turnover_t = abs(pos_t − pos_prev) (0, 1, or 2), and cost = turnover_t × evidence_cost_bps × 1e-4.

The key insight for Point 3: 0.1 bp and 0.01 bp will constrain the model less than 1 bp — and that is exactly the point. If the model is currently flat or near-flat at 1 bp, lowering the cost removes the penalty that was suppressing it and lets the model express whatever signal it has learned. The three regimes we want to see:

CostWhat it tells us
1 bpCurrent state: model is flat / near-flat → cost is masking signal, or there is no signal.
0.1 bpModel expresses some signal but may still be dominated by cost if it over-trades.
0.01 bpCost is negligible → model's unmasked behavior is revealed. If it still over-trades, the reward structure (not the cost) is the problem.

The cost curve is the most important diagnostic. A 3-point cost sweep (0.01, 0.1, 1 bp) × {R0, R4-v1} × {multiple seeds} gives a cost-response surface:

  • Flat across cost → architecture has no signal (cost is irrelevant).
  • Improves as cost drops → cost was masking signal; architecture has real edge at low cost.
  • Worsens as cost drops → model is over-trading; reward structure is wrong.

Recommendation (proposed):

  1. Keep 1 bp as the economic anchor — it is the "real-world" cost we care about.
  2. Add 0.1 bp and 0.01 bp as diagnostic cells — they reveal the model's unmasked behavior.
  3. Report the cost-response curve as a first-class artifact — it should be in every ablation summary.
  4. Do NOT use 0.01 bp as a promotion criterion — it is a diagnostic, not an economic target. Promotion still requires positive net at 1 bp on the sealed final test.

On "punishing overtrading properly": the current net_return objective already penalizes turnover through optimization_cost_bps. The knob to watch is whether the model's turnover-to-PnL ratio improves as we change the cost. If the model is trading 20+ times per episode at 0.01 bp but only 3 at 1 bp, the reward structure is encouraging over-trading at low cost — and we should add an explicit turnover penalty (a fixed per-trade cost that does NOT scale with evidence_cost_bps) to discourage position-flipping even at low cost.

3 · Architecture test vs economic test — the "stable, semi-profitable solver" goal (Point 4)

User framing: "We are mixing the two, but ultimately we are trying to get the most stable, semi-profitable solver (not even economic return, but proven solver capability) so we can couple the proposer later. Simply changing/mutating TaskSpec without knowing solver profitability only adds more variables to tweak and therefore harder to attribute to training. Can you take stock of the amount of knobs we are actually turning and thus have to isolate?"

Answer: I agree the two questions are being conflated, and the fix is to name them and sequence them.

The two questions:

Architecture test: does R4-v1 (or R1) have better attribution than R0?Paired 95% net-return interval on a frozen validation panelPositive lower bound
Economic test: does the solver make money at 1 bp on untouched data?Net return on the sealed final testPositive net + positive Sharpe + multi-seed

The "most stable, semi-profitable solver" goal is a third thing — a capability floor that says: "this solver is good enough to learn from the proposer, even if it is not yet profitable." That floor should be defined explicitly:

  • Stability: deterministic post-restore evaluation, exact fresh replay, block-aware uncertainty, no terminal-exploit mutations, checkpoint/replay parity.
  • Semi-profitability: positive net at 0.1 bp on the frozen validation panel, positive Sharpe, and turnover below a pre-declared threshold (e.g., ≤ 5 per episode).

If we change the training budget, the cost, the learning rate, the window, or the PBT setting between R0 and R4-v1, the attribution is confounded and the 95% interval is meaningless.

Concrete recommendation (proposed):

  1. Freeze the 14 non-solver knobs in a single manifest with hashes.
  2. Change only the solver state mode (and the architecture: R0 vs R4-v1).
  3. Run each cell under the same training budget, cost, seeds, and PBT setting.
  4. Report the 95% paired net-return interval on the frozen validation panel.
  5. Only then consider the "capability floor" for proposer coupling.

4 · The 70/15/15 split and the "oldest → newest" training sequence (Point 5)

User framing: "Splitting the same way for all is brilliant, especially since we can attempt to start anew and go from oldest → newest in the split format, thus giving us that retrospective bet that maybe it can generalize and follow the trend chronologically!"

Answer: the split is already the right choice, and the oldest→newest training sequence is a strong additional safeguard — but it is a design choice we need to make explicitly, not implicitly.

Why the 70/15/15 chronological split is right:

  • Chronological order is the correct train/val/test split for time-series data. Random splitting would leak future information into the training set.
  • The 15% validation is for selection and the 15% final test is for the one-shot promotion gate. The final test is sealed — it is never used for training or selection.

The oldest→newest training sequence (a.k.a. "expanding window" or "walk-forward") — this is a stronger variant of the chronological split. Instead of training once on the first 70% and evaluating on the next 15%, you:

  1. Train on the first 70% (oldest), evaluate on the next 15%.
  2. Train on the first 85% (70% + 15% of validation), evaluate on the remaining 15% (final test).
  3. Repeat as new data becomes available.

The "retrospective bet" you described is the key insight: if the model trained on 2022–2024 data can still produce positive net on 2025–2026 data, it has learned a generalizable pattern, not a regime-specific artifact. That is the strongest form of the "no leakage" guarantee.

However — the current dataset has only ~4.9 years of data (2022-01-03 → 2026-09-22). The oldest→newest sequence is:

  • Train: 2022-01-03 → ~2024-05 (70%)
  • Val: ~2024-05 → ~2025-05 (15%)
  • Test: ~2025-05 → 2026-09-22 (15%)

That is a reasonable sequence, but the "retrospective bet" only works if:

  1. The model is not overfit to the 2022–2024 regime. The 4.9-year span includes multiple market regimes (2022 bear, 2023 recovery, 2024 volatility, 2025 growth). If the model is trained on 2022–2024 and evaluated on 2025, it may be generalizing or it may be regime-mismatched.
  2. The 15% validation panel is truly non-overlapping. The current split_purge_rows=200 is a good start, but we should increase the purge (e.g., to 500–1000 rows) to ensure no temporal leakage between train and val.
  3. The final test is truly sealed. If the model has ever seen the 2025–2026 data during training, the final test is contaminated.

Concrete recommendation (proposed):

  1. Freeze the 70/15/15 split with purge ≥ 500 rows between train and val.
  2. Record the exact panel boundaries (start/end timestamps) in the manifest.
  3. Train oldest→newest — this is the "retrospective bet."
  4. Report the per-year net return (2022, 2023, 2024, 2025, 2026) as a regime diagnostic. If the model is profitable in 2022–2024 but not in 2025–2026, it is regime-mismatched, not generalizable.
  5. Keep the final test sealed until the retrospective bet has been run and the 95% interval is qualified.

5 · Window size vs. cost: the 60-minute input shape (Point 6)

User framing: "The input shape takes 60 min (60 × 5 into the MLP flattened + FiLM + solver state), but I absolutely like matching the window to the window structure. How did you measure that 2-hour to 1 bp cost? Maybe we can keep that proportion and do window ablations."

Answer: the 60-minute window is a good starting point, but we should test the window-to-cost ratio explicitly, not assume it.

The current setup:

  • Window: 60 bars = 60 minutes
  • Horizons: 96, 128, 160, 200 bars = 96–200 minutes
  • Cost: 1 bp per round-trip
  • Training: iters=40 → ~192,000 steps

The window-to-cost ratio question: the model is being asked to predict the next 96–200 minutes of price movement, but it is only given 60 minutes of history. That is a 1.6–3.3× forward horizon relative to the input window. If the model is paying 1 bp per round-trip to change position, it needs to be confident enough to justify that trade. The window-to-cost ratio should be:

  • If the window is too short relative to the horizon, the model is making decisions on insufficient signal → over-trades.
  • If the window is too long relative to the horizon, the model is making decisions on stale signal → under-trades.
  • The cost should scale with the decision frequency. A 1 bp cost is reasonable if the model trades 1–5 times per episode; it is too high if the model trades 20+ times per episode.

The 2-hour to 1 bp measurement (the user's question): I did not measure a specific "2-hour to 1 bp" ratio in the current code. The 1 bp cost is arbitrary — it was set to a round number that is in the ballpark of typical SIP fees. The honest measurement is:

  1. Measure the model's turnover (position changes per episode) at 1 bp, 0.1 bp, and 0.01 bp.
  2. Calculate the cost-to-PnL ratio at each cost level.
  3. If the model's turnover drops to near-zero at 1 bp but rises to 10+ at 0.01 bp, the 1 bp cost is too high for the model's decision frequency, and we should either (a) increase the window to give it more signal, or (b) reduce the cost, or (c) accept that the model is not ready to trade at 1 bp.

Window ablation (the user's idea): this is a good next step. The current window (60) is a single point. A window ablation would test:

WindowInput dimsWhat it tells us
30 bars (30 min)150Does the model need less signal?
60 bars (60 min)300Current state
120 bars (2 hr)600Does the model need more signal?
240 bars (4 hr)1200Does the model need even more signal?

The recommendation: test the window ablation in parallel with the cost sweep. A 4-window × 3-cost × 2-seed = 24 cells is a manageable budget if the training budget is kept at the current iters=40 smoke level. The goal is to find the window-cost pair that maximizes net return at 1 bp, subject to the constraint that the model is not over-trading (turnover ≤ 5 per episode).

6 · market_relative_v1 vs. market_relative_v2 (Point 7)

User framing: "Can you expand on how your proposed market_relative_v2 could work? I like our original approach as you said — it's training on patterns and not actual prices — but I guess we have to provide this in some form too!"

The current market_relative_v1 transform (grounding):

def _market_relative_window(self, window: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    """Create bounded, fitted-state-free OHLCV features for portable checkpoints."""
    close_ref = float(window[-1, self.cols.index(self.close_col)])
    price_scale = max(abs(close_ref), 1e-12)
    for index, column in enumerate(self.cols):
        if column.upper() in {"OPEN", "HIGH", "LOW", "CLOSE"}:
            window[:, index] = window[:, index] / price_scale - 1.0
        elif column.upper() == "VOLUME":
            window[:, index] = np.log1p(np.maximum(window[:, index], 0.0))
        else:
            mean = float(np.mean(window[:, index]))
            std = float(np.std(window[:, index]))
            window[:, index] = (window[:, index] - mean) / max(std, 1e-8)
    return np.clip(_sanitize(window), -10.0, 10.0)

What it does:

  • Prices (O/H/L/C) are divided by the current close and 1 is subtracted → the model sees relative returns (e.g., "this open was 0.5% below the current close").
  • Volume is log1p-normalized → compresses the dynamic range.
  • All features are z-scored (mean 0, std 1) and clipped to ±10 → prevents outliers from exploding the input.
  • No fitted parameters are used → the transform is state-free and portable across checkpoints (a key requirement for the replay contract).

The problem with market_relative_v1: the transform is state-free (good for portability) but not scale-invariant across the full dataset. It normalizes each window independently using the current close as the reference. This means:

  • A window in 2022 (when AAPL was ~$30) and a window in 2025 (when AAPL is ~$200) are both normalized to the same scale (±10). This is good for the model to learn patterns rather than absolute prices.
  • But the model cannot see the absolute level of the market. If the model needs to know "the market is in a high-volatility regime," the market_relative_v1 transform does not provide that.

The proposed market_relative_v2 transform: the key idea is to add a second, fitted normalization layer that preserves the state-free property of market_relative_v1 while exposing a market-level signal that the model can use to detect regime changes.

def _market_relative_window_v2(self, window: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    """Add a fitted market-level context to the state-free v1 transform."""
    # Step 1: Apply v1 (state-free, portable).
    window_v1 = self._market_relative_window(window)

    # Step 2: Add a *fitted* market-level context (e.g., realized volatility over
    # the last N bars, using a *frozen* training-time statistic).
    # This is NOT computed from the current window — it is a *precomputed*
    # feature that is loaded from a manifest, so it does not leak future info.
    # Example: realized volatility = std of log-returns over the last 252 bars
    # (1 year), computed once and stored in the dataset manifest.
    if not hasattr(self, "_fitted_volatility"):
        raise ValueError("market_relative_v2 requires a fitted volatility manifest")
    vol = self._fitted_volatility
    window_v1 = np.concatenate([window_v1, [vol] * len(window_v1[0])])

    # Step 3: Re-clip to ±10.
    return np.clip(_sanitize(window_v1), -10.0, 10.0)

Key properties of market_relative_v2:

  • State-free: the fitted volatility is precomputed and stored in the manifest → no leakage, no checkpoint incompatibility.
  • Adds regime signal: the model can now see "the market is in a high-volatility regime" and adjust its position sizing accordingly.
  • Backward-compatible: the v1 dimensions are a prefix of the v2 dimensions → old checkpoints can be loaded into v2 with zero-init padding.

The recommendation:

  • Keep market_relative_v1 as the default — it is the state-free, portable baseline.
  • Add market_relative_v2 as an opt-in factor — it adds regime signal but requires a fitted manifest.
  • Compare v1 vs v2 in a controlled ablation — hold everything else constant (training budget, cost, seeds, window) and change only the transform.
  • Do NOT promote v2 until the v1 vs v2 comparison is qualified — v2 is a hypothesis, not a settled architecture.

7 · The "movement" penalty and turnover (Point 8)

User framing: "I guess you are right, but here it gets tricky — we have to be careful with this. Technically each decision is a 'movement,' so that's why we are penalizing."

Answer: the penalty is correct, but we need to make sure it is proportional to the movement, not binary.

The current penalty (grounding):

turnover_t = abs(float(self.pos) - prev_pos)  # 0, 1 or 2

What this means:

  • Flat → Flat: turnover_t = 0 → no penalty.
  • Flat → Long: turnover_t = 1 → 1 unit of cost.
  • Long → Long: turnover_t = 0 → no penalty.
  • Long → Short: turnover_t = 2 → 2 units of cost (because the position changes from +1 to -1, a full round-trip).

The "movement" framing is correct — every position change is a movement, and every movement should be penalized. The question is: is the penalty proportional to the movement?

The answer: yes, it is. A 1→2 change (Long → Short) is twice as costly as a 0→1 change (Flat → Long), because it involves two position changes (Long → Flat, then Flat → Short). This is the correct economic model — a round-trip trade costs two one-way fees.

The subtlety: the evidence_cost_bps scales the penalty. If the model is currently over-trading (e.g., 20 position changes per episode), the penalty at 1 bp is 0.20% — which is substantial. At 0.01 bp, the same over-trading is only 0.002% — which is negligible. So the cost knob is the right tool for discouraging over-trading, but only if the model is actually over-trading.

The recommendation:

  • Keep the turnover_t penalty as-is — it is the correct economic model.
  • Measure the model's turnover at 1 bp, 0.1 bp, and 0.01 bp.
  • If the model is over-trading at 0.01 bp (turnover > 10 per episode), the problem is not the cost knob — it is the reward structure. The model is being encouraged to trade by the PnL signal, and the cost is too small to stop it. In that case, add an explicit turnover penalty that does NOT scale with evidence_cost_bps (e.g., a fixed 0.5 bp per position change, added on top of the evidence_cost_bps).
  • If the model is under-trading at 1 bp (turnover < 2 per episode), the problem is the cost knob — it is too high, and the model is being discouraged from trading. Lower the cost to 0.1 bp or 0.01 bp to see if the model expresses more signal.

8 · The family roadmap: Discrete(3) → richer action spaces (Point 9)

User framing: "Technically we are only asking 'flat, long, or short?' and attempting to modulate the solver state to the input shape. But we were trying to discretise this particular part of trading as task family 00 (directional decisions). 01 is vol breakout, 02 is mean reversion pairs. With this in mind, maybe for the other families that were planned to have Discrete(3), we could actually expand to this, as it simulates these things. But again, the online play (Phase C) will handle this eventually — not tested fully yet, and still experimental. Can you scaffold how that would look?"

Answer: the current Discrete(3) is the right starting point for family 00, but the roadmap to richer action spaces for families 01–02 is a good long-term investment — and it is exactly what the masterplan anticipates.

The current state (grounding from the masterplan):

  • Family 00: directional (short/flat/long) — Discrete(3). ✅
  • Family 01: volatility breakout — Discrete(3) planned.
  • Family 02: mean reversion pairs — Discrete(3) planned.
  • Family 03: trade management — Discrete(3) planned.
  • Family 04: abstention/WAIT semantics — Discrete(3) planned.

The problem with a uniform Discrete(3) action space: the current Discrete(3) action space assumes that every family makes the same three decisions: short, flat, long. But that is not true for families 01–02:

  • Family 01 (volatility breakout): the model should be able to enter a position, set a stop-loss, and exit. That is more than 3 actions — it is a sequence of actions.
  • Family 02 (mean reversion pairs): the model should be able to enter a pair, hold, and exit — again, a sequence, not a single decision.
  • Family 03 (trade management): the model should be able to adjust position size, set a stop-loss, and exit — a continuous decision space, not discrete.

The scaffold for richer action spaces:

Option A: Discrete(3) with a conditional action space

The model still outputs 3 actions (short/flat/long), but each action has a payload:

  • Short: {position_size, stop_loss, exit_trigger}
  • Flat: {position_size, stop_loss, exit_trigger}
  • Long: {position_size, stop_loss, exit_trigger}

This is a hybrid discrete/continuous action space. The discrete part is the direction, and the continuous part is the trade management. The model learns both in one policy.

Option B: Discrete(5) with explicit WAIT and EXIT

Expand the action space to 5:

  • Short
  • Flat
  • Long
  • WAIT (hold current position, do not trade)
  • EXIT (liquidate current position)

This is a cleaner discrete space that explicitly models abstention (family 04) and liquidation (family 03). The model can now choose to wait rather than being forced to either enter or exit.

Option C: Discrete(3) + a separate trade management policy

The model outputs 3 actions (short/flat/long) for the directional decision, and a separate policy outputs the trade management parameters (position_size, stop_loss, exit_trigger). This is a multi-head architecture — the masterplan already anticipates this ("a shared recurrent trunk with family-specific policy/value heads").

The recommendation:

  1. Keep Discrete(3) for family 00 — it is the simplest and the most directly testable.
  2. Add WAIT and EXIT to the action space for families 01–04 — this is Option B and is the lowest-risk expansion.
  3. Scaffold the multi-head architecture for families 01–03 — this is Option C and is the long-term investment.
  4. Do NOT expand the action space until the Discrete(3) baseline is promoted — the masterplan is explicit: "families 00–02 may share Discrete(3) output width without settling input schemas, task semantics, reward assignments, or state ownership."

The concrete scaffold:

# Option B: Discrete(5) with WAIT and EXIT
# 0 = SHORT, 1 = FLAT, 2 = LONG, 3 = WAIT, 4 = EXIT

# The model outputs a single integer in {0, 1, 2, 3, 4}.
# The environment interprets:
#   0: enter/hold short
#   1: hold flat
#   2: enter/hold long
#   3: wait (no change, no cost)
#   4: liquidate (close position, incur liquidation cost)

# This is a *minimal* expansion that adds the two most important
# trade-management actions without changing the discrete action space.

The Phase C connection: the user is right that Phase C will handle this eventually. The DecisionGate, RiskEngine, and Executor shell in Phase C will translate the policy's action into a concrete trade (with position sizing, stop-loss, and execution logic). The Discrete(3) → Discrete(5) → multi-head evolution is the training-time version of that translation. The key is to keep the training-time action space simple (Discrete(3) or Discrete(5)) and let Phase C handle the complexity of position sizing, stop-loss, and execution.
